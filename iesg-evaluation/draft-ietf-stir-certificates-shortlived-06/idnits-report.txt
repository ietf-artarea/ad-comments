7	         Short-Lived Certificates for Secure Telephone Identity
8	               draft-ietf-stir-certificates-shortlived-06

10	Abstract

12	   When certificates are used as credentials to attest the assignment of
13	   ownership of telephone numbers, some mechanism is required to provide
14	   certificate freshness.  This document specifies short-lived
15	   certificates as a means of guaranteeing certificate freshness for
16	   secure telephone identity (STIR), potentially relying on the
17	   Automated Certificate Management Environment (ACME) or similar
18	   mechanisms to allow signers to acquire certificates as needed.

68	1.  Introduction

70	   The STIR problem statement [RFC7340] discusses many attacks on the
71	   telephone network that are enabled by impersonation, including
72	   various forms of robocalling, voicemail hacking, and swatting.  One
73	   of the most important components of a system to prevent impersonation
74	   is the implementation of credentials that identify the parties who
75	   control telephone numbers.  The STIR certificates [RFC8226]
76	   specification describes a credential system based on [X.509] version
77	   3 certificates in accordance with [RFC5280] for that purpose.  Those
78	   credentials can then be used by STIR authentication services
79	   [RFC8224] to sign PASSporT objects [RFC8225] carried in a SIP
80	   [RFC3261] request.

82	   The STIR certificates document specifies an extension to X.509 that
83	   defines a Telephony Number (TN) Authorization List that may be
84	   included by certification authorities in certificates.  This
85	   extension provides additional information that relying parties can
86	   use when validating transactions with the certificate: either in the
87	   form of Service Provider Codes (SPCs) or telephone numbers.
88	   Telephone numbers or number ranges are used in delegate STIR
89	   certificates [RFC9060].  When a SIP request arrives at a terminating
90	   administrative domain, for example, the calling number attested by
91	   the SIP request can be compared to the TN Authorization List of the
92	   delegate certificate that signed the request to determine if the
93	   caller is authorized to use that calling number in SIP.

95	   No specific recommendation is made in the STIR certificates document
96	   for a means of determining the freshness of certificates with a TN
97	   Authorization List.  This document specifies how short-lived
98	   certificates could be used as a means of preserving that freshness.
99	   Short-lived certificates also have a number of other desirable
100	   properties that fulfill important operational requirements for
101	   network operators.  A mechanism such as the Automated Certificate
102	   Management Environment (ACME) [RFC8555] could be leveraged to manage
103	   these short-lived certificates, as well as various web-based
104	   interfaces or other out-of-band mechanisms.  The interaction of STIR
105	   with ACME has already been explored in [RFC9448], so it provides a
106	   potentially attractive way of delivering short-lived certificates.

108	   As the use of short-lived certificates described below involves a
109	   certificate chain included by-value in the PASSporT header, this
110	   document specifies the use of "x5c" in the PASSporT header when
111	   short-lived certificates are present.

121	3.  Short-lived certificates for STIR

123	   While there is no easy definition of what constitutes a "short-lived"
124	   certificate, the term typically refers to certificates that are valid
125	   only for days or even hours, as opposed to the months or years common
126	   in traditional public key infrastructures.  When the private keying
127	   material associated with a certificate with an expiry of months or
128	   years is compromised by an adversary, relying parties must review
129	   certificate revocation lists (CRLs) or access real-time status
130	   information with protocols such as OCSP to learn that the certificate
131	   has been revoked.  Short-lived certificates offer an alternative
132	   where, if compromised, certificates will shortly expire anyway, so
133	   CRLs and OCSP are less meaningful.  Such certificates cannot be
134	   cached for a long term by relying parties, minimizing their value to
135	   attackers.

137	   One of the additional benefits of using short-lived certificates is
138	   that they do not require relying parties to perform any certificate
139	   freshness check.  The trade-off is that the signer must acquire new
140	   certificates frequently, so the cost of round-trip times to the
141	   certification authority is paid on the signer's side rather than the
142	   verifier's side; however, in environments where many parties may rely
143	   on a single certificate, or at least where a single certificate will
144	   be used to sign many transactions during its short lifetime, the
145	   overall architecture will incur fewer round-trip times to the
146	   certification authority and thus less processing delay.

148	   In the STIR context, the TN Authorization List defined in [RFC8226]
149	   adds a new wrinkle to the behavior of short-lived certificates,
150	   especially when the TN Authorization List is populated with telephone
151	   numbers or number ranges instead of Service Provider Codes (SPCs).  A
152	   subject may have authority over multiple telephone numbers, but a
153	   particular short-lived certificate issued to that subject could
154	   attest the authority over all, some, or just one of those telephone
155	   numbers.  Short-lived certificates permit a more on-demand
156	   certification process, where subjects acquire certificates as needed,
157	   potentially in reaction to calls being placed.  A STIR authentication
158	   service could even acquire a new certificate on a per-call basis that
159	   can only sign for the calling party number of the call in question,
160	   as it would expire immediately thereafter.  At the other end of the
161	   spectrum, a large enterprise service provider could acquire a
162	   certificate valid for millions of numbers, but expire the certificate
163	   after a very short duration - on the order of hours - to reduce the
164	   risk that the certificate would be compromised.

166	   This inherent flexibility in the short-lived certificate architecture
167	   would also permit authentication services to implement very narrow
168	   policies for certificate usage.  A large service provider who wanted
169	   to avoid revealing which phone numbers they controlled, for example,
170	   could provide no information in the certificate that signs a call
171	   other than just the single telephone number that corresponds to the
172	   calling party's number.  How frequently the service provider feels
173	   that they need to expire that certificate and acquire a new one is
174	   entirely a matter of local policy.  This makes it much harder for
175	   entities monitoring signatures over calls to guess who owns which
176	   numbers, and provides a much more complicated threat surface for
177	   attackers trying to compromise the service.

179	4.  Certificate conveyance with 'x5c'

181	   In order to reduce the burden on verification services, an
182	   authentication service could also piggyback a short-lived certificate
183	   onto the PASSporT, so that no network lookup and consequent round-
184	   trip delay would be required on the terminating side to acquire the
185	   new certificate.  In particular, the poor cacheability of short-lived
186	   certificates may require frequent fetches of certificates via the
187	   "x5u" PASSporT header element when relying parties validate
188	   PASSporTs.

190	   As an optimization, this specification specifies the conveyance of
191	   the certificate chain for a short-lived certificate via the "x5c" JWS
192	   header element ([RFC7515] Section 4.1.6).  The "x5c" element contains
193	   a base64 encoded PEM representation of the certificate chain.  STIR
194	   Verification service implementations compliant with this
195	   specification MUST support the "x5c" element; authentication services
196	   MUST use the "x5c" format for PASSporTs signed by certificates with a
197	   valid lifetime shorter than one week.  The presence of x5c creates
198	   PASSporT objects that are considerably larger than typical RFC8225
199	   tokens, and the longer the certificate chain, the larger the PASSporT
200	   header will be.  But provided the certificate chain leads to a
201	   trusted certification authority, "x5c" precludes the need for a
202	   round-trip time before validation at the STIR verification service.
203	   The root certificate SHOULD NOT be included in the chain, though it
204	   may be required in some deployment environments.

259	5.  Certificate Acquisition with ACME

261	   One of the primary challenges facing short-lived certificates is
262	   building an operational system that allows signers to acquire new
263	   certificates and put them to immediate use.  ACME [RFC8555] is
264	   designed for exactly this purpose.  After a client registers with an
265	   ACME server, and the authority of the client for the names in
266	   question is established (through means such as [RFC9448]), the client
267	   can at any time apply for a certificate to be issued by sending an
268	   appropriate JSON request to the server.  That request will contain a
269	   CSR [RFC2986] indicating the intended scope of authority as well the
270	   validity interval of the certificate in question.  Ultimately, this
271	   will enable the client to download the certificate from a certificate
272	   URL designated by the server.

274	   ACME is based on the concept that clients establish accounts at an
275	   ACME server, and that through challenges, the server learns which
276	   identifiers it will issue for certificates requested for an account.
277	   Any given certificate issued for an account can be for just one of
278	   those identifiers, or potentially for more: this is determined by the
279	   CSR that an ACME client creates for a particular order.  Thus, a
280	   service provider with authority for millions of identifiers - that
281	   is, millions of telephone numbers - could create a CSR for an ACME
282	   order that requests a certificate only associated with one of those
283	   telephone numbers if it so desired.  The same would be true of
284	   certificates based on Service Provider Codes (SPCs) as described in
285	   [RFC8226]: a service provider might have just one SPC or perhaps
286	   many.  ACME thus provides flexibility to clients requesting
287	   certificates to determine how much of their authority they want to
288	   invest in any given certificate.

290	   [RFC9448] uses the Authority Token Challenge (ATC) framework of
291	   [RFC9447] to generate tokens that are provided to the CA in response
292	   to ACME challenges.  For a usage with short-term certificates, it may
293	   make sense for the ATC tokens to have a relatively long expiry, so
294	   that the ACME client does not have to constantly return to the Token
295	   Authority for new tokens.  This could potentially be used with the
296	   ACME STAR [RFC8739] mechanism as well.

298	6.  Ooperational Considerations

300	   Short-lived certificates shift the cost of certificate freshness from
301	   the verification side to the authentication service, which makes the
302	   reachability of the certification authority and its ACME interface
303	   operationally critical to call signing.  Operators SHOULD renew
304	   certificates in advance of expiry with enough lead time to absorb CA
305	   latency and transient failures, recognizing that the shorter the
306	   certificate lifetime, the tighter that renewal margin becomes.
307	   Operators should stagger renewals and apply backoff on failure rather
308	   than retry aggressively against a busy CA, and the ACME STAR
309	   mechanism [RFC8739] can remove the client from the synchronous
310	   acquisition path by pre-issuing renewed certificates.  Useful signals
311	   to monitor include renewal success rate and lead-time margin, ACME
312	   error rates, and the rate of verification failures attributable to
313	   expired certificates, which indicates a renewal pipeline falling
314	   behind.

316	   Conveying the certificate chain in "x5c" trades a network round-trip
317	   for increased message size: the chain travels in every PASSporT,
318	   enlarging the SIP Identity header by roughly the size of the chain.
319	   This is a deliberate exchange of originating-side overhead for the
320	   elimination of call-time fetches on the terminating side, but
321	   operators should be aware that larger headers can interact poorly
322	   with SIP transport, including fragmentation over UDP and
323	   intermediaries that impose header-size limits.  The size cost is
324	   minimized by keeping the TNAuthorizationList as small as the
325	   deployment allows, keeping certificate chains short, and omitting the
326	   root certificate from the chain where deployment conditions permit.

332	8.  Privacy Considerations

334	   Short-lived certificates provide attractive privacy properties when
335	   compared to real-time status protocols like OCSP, which require
336	   relying parties to perform a network query that can reveal a great
337	   deal about the source and destination of communications.  For STIR,
338	   these problems are compounded by the presence of the TN Authorization
339	   List extension to certificates.  Short-lived certificates can
340	   minimize the data that needs to appear in the TN Authorization List,
341	   and consequently reduce the amount of information about the caller
342	   leaked by certificate usage to an amount equal to what is leaked by
343	   the call signaling itself.

345	9.  Security Considerations

347	   For further information on certificate security and practices, see
348	   [RFC5280], in particular its Security Considerations.
349	   Implementations should note the Security Considerations of [RFC8226]
350	   for guidance on STIR certificate usage.  The Security Considerations
351	   of [RFC8555] are relevant to the use of ACME to acquire short-lived
352	   certificates, as are those of [RFC8739].
